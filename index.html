Grasping the Future

Joint 3D Hand–Object Forecasting from Internet Videos

Forty seconds: forecasts in 3D, three possible futures, one clip asked seven questions, the data, and robot hands following a forecast.

Grasping-the-Future predicts how hands and objects move together in 3D. Show it half a second of someone handling an object, and it predicts the next four and a half seconds: where the wrists go, how the fingers bend, and how the object moves and turns. The same model answers other questions too: how the object moves given the hands, how the hands move given the object, how to reach a goal, and what happened in a gap. It learns from 62,562 interactions we rebuilt in 3D from internet videos, plus eight motion-capture datasets.

Abstract

To interact with an object, we need to anticipate both how it will move and how our hands should move with it. Human videos capture these relationships across everyday activities, offering a natural source for learning to predict future interactions. Learning from them requires recovering motion from completed interactions and using it to anticipate what could happen next. We present Grasping-the-Future, a unified generative model of joint 3D hand-object motion, including wrist motion, finger articulation, and object position and orientation. Through masked spatiotemporal completion and flow matching, we train a single model for several prediction tasks: forecasting future interactions, predicting hand motion given object motion or vice versa, generating both trajectories toward a desired final object pose, and completing partially observed interactions. We explicitly model hand-object relations and incorporate contact constraints to help preserve grasp relationships during motion. To obtain 3D supervision from internet videos, we develop a scalable pipeline that combines pretrained perception models with joint geometric optimization to reconstruct completed interactions. We combine these recovered trajectories with annotated interaction datasets as training data for all tasks. We demonstrate strong performance across all tasks, outperforming released forecasters on datasets with 3D annotations. Moreover, we show geometric and physical plausibility of the predicted trajectories by retargeting them to a robotic embodiment.

Data

From internet video to 3D.

Internet videos have no 3D labels, so we make them. Off-the-shelf models estimate the hands, the object and the depth in each frame. An optimization joins them into one consistent 3D motion. Checks throw out every clip that doesn't hold up. On egocentric video, the result is as accurate as HOPformer, a recent model built for egocentric images; from third-person views, its hand error is about nine times lower and its object pose error about a quarter lower.

    Model

    Fill in the grid.

    The model sees an interaction as a grid. It keeps the cells it is given, starts the rest from noise, and turns the noise into motion in ten steps.

    The figure from the paper
    • One grid for every taskEach moment has five tokens: the two hands, the object, and where each hand sits relative to the object. A task is just a choice of which cells to give.
    • Looking along time and across handsTwelve transformer blocks look along time, across the hands and the object, and over the whole grid. They also see the scene and the shapes of the object and hands.
    • Grasps that holdTraining also checks the finished motion: the hand has to stay attached to the object, and whole sets of futures are scored together.

    Closer to what actually happened.

    Errors in millimetres on our benchmark of 331 five-second windows: 256 from motion capture and 75 from internet video. Each method runs on its own inputs, and every prediction is scored with the same code.

    Compared with released forecasters

    Final position after 4.5 s, mm, lower is better

    A method that draws several futures (×N) is scored by the energy score of all of them: their average error minus half their average distance from each other. It is lowest when the futures are both close to what happened and as spread out as the real possibilities; for a method that makes one prediction it is simply its error. Ours draws 20 futures and uses estimated depth here.

    Robots

    From forecast to robot hands.

    We hand each forecast to a simulated robot hand and check whether it moves the object along the predicted path. Copying the wrist and fingertips rarely works. Physics-aware retargeting with SPIDER works for most forecasts.
